# 🌕 Running a Moon base: the 5 big jobs 🚀

Imagine you are in charge of a small base near the Moon's south pole. There is no air outside, no shop nearby, and Earth is 384,000 km away. Here are the five jobs you have to keep track of.

## 1. 🫁 Life support: "Can we breathe, drink and stay warm?" 💧🌡️

💡 What it means: the base is like a sealed bubble. Machines inside make air, clean water and keep the right temperature. If they stop, the crew is in trouble.

### 🔬 Real facts:

- 🫁 Every astronaut breathes in about 0.8 kg of oxygen a day, roughly a big bottle of fizzy drink in weight, but as gas. They also breathe out about 1 kg of carbon dioxide, which machines must remove.
- 💧 Each astronaut needs about 2.5 litres of water a day for drinking and mixing into food.
- ⚡ On the space station, oxygen is made by splitting water with electricity. Water is made of hydrogen and oxygen, and electricity pulls them apart. One NASA model says this takes about 14 kWh of electricity for each kg of oxygen, about what a home uses in half a day.
- ♻️ The space station recycles water again and again, even from sweat and urine!
- 🪨 Moon soil is about 40-45% oxygen, locked inside the rocks. Scientists are testing ways to get it out.

⚖️ Why it's a hard choice: making air uses power. If you take power away from the air machine to do science, the air tank slowly drops. "Saving power today costs oxygen tomorrow."

## 2. ☢️ Radiation shielding: "Are we protected from invisible space rays?" 🛡️

💡 What it means: on Earth, our air and the planet's magnetic field act like a giant invisible umbrella. The Moon has no umbrella, so astronauts get hit by tiny fast particles from space. You can't see or feel them, but too many can harm the body.

### ☀️ Two kinds of rays:

- 🌌 Cosmic rays come from far outside our solar system. They are always there and so powerful that they pass through walls and spacesuits almost as if they weren't there. Walls only stop about 1 in 10.
- 🌞 Solar storms are sudden bursts of particles from the Sun. They are rare, but can give a big dose in just a few hours. The good news is that they are easy to block with a shelter.

### 🔬 Real facts:

- 📏 Radiation dose is measured in millisieverts (mSv).
- 🚀 On NASA's Artemis I trip around the Moon (2022), detectors measured about 1 mSv a day. A flight from London to New York gives about 0.08 mSv, so one day near the Moon is like about 12 long plane flights.
- 🌕 On the Moon's surface you get about half that, because the Moon blocks everything coming from below you,.
- ⚠️ NASA's limits: under 600 mSv in an astronaut's whole career, and no more than 250 mSv from one solar storm.
- ⏱️ The fastest storm particles can arrive tens of minutes after a flare. Slower storm clouds take from about 15 hours to a few days,
- 🛡️ On Artemis I, NASA worked out that hiding in a shelter made of supply bags would keep even a big storm to about 150 mSy, under the limit,
- 💧 Water is one of the best shields. It's full of hydrogen, which is great at stopping particles. A shelter that haives a storm's dose for 4 astronauts weighs about 1.3-1.6 tonnes, as much as a car!
- ☀️ The Sun has busy and quiet years in an 11-year cycle. Busy Sun means more storms. Quiet Sun means more cosmic rays, about twice as many. There is no "safe" time.

### 🧑‍🚀 NASA does two things, just like your game will:

a. 🗺️ Plan: use computer models to predict the dose before launch. On Artemis I the prediction was within 3-4% of the real measurement!

b. 📡 Measure: watch live with sensors and alarms, and shelter if a storm comes,

⚖️ Why it's a hard choice: shielding is heavy, and every kilogram you launch means something else stays behind. Hiding in the shelter keeps you safe but stops your moonwalks and science for that day.

## 3. ⚡ Power budgeting: "How do we share out the electricity?" 🔋

💡 What it means: think of the base's battery like pocket money. Everything costs some: the air machine, heaters, radios, lights, science experiments. You can't buy everything, so you choose.

### 🔬 Real facts:

- ☀️ Sunlight at the Moon gives about 1,361 watts on every square metre, and the best space solar panels turn about 30% of that into electricity.
- 🌑 Near the equator, the Moon's night lasts about 14 Earth days, and it gets as cold as -173 °C. Keeping a base running on batteries through that night would need about 22,000 kg of batteries, as heavy as about 4 elephants!
- 🌕 That's why NASA is going to the south pole. There the Sun never rises high. It slides around the horizon like a torch circling the room. On the best ridges it shines about 9 days out of 10.
- 🌗 Even there, in polar winter, dark spells come every month and last from a few hours to a few days. In polar summer, the best spots can stay sunny for months.
- 🌘 About twice a year, Earth's shadow covers the Moon (an eclipse), and even sunny ridges go dark for a few hours.
- 🔆 Because the Sun is so low, NASA is testing tall solar panels standing up to about 10-20 m high, like flagpoles, so hills don't shade them.
- 🌡️ Even an empty base needs about 1.5 kW of heating in the dark, the same as a big electric heater left on.
- ☢️ Later, NASA plans a small nuclear power station of about 40 kW, enough for about 30 homes, that works day and night.

⚖️ Why it's a hard choice: power you spend on science today is power you can't save for the next dark spell.

## 4. 🥬 Food production: "What will the crew eat?" 🌱

💡 What it means: right now, astronauts don't grow their dinner. All their food is packed on Earth and sent up with them. Growing food in space is still an experiment.

### 🔬 Real facts:

- 🥫 Space food is packed to last a long time, and much of it just needs water added.
- 🥬 of Astronauts have grown lettuce and other plants on the space station as experiments,
- 🌱 NASA plans a plant experiment called LEAF to see how plants grow on the Moon,
- 🪴 Scientists on Earth have grown small plants in real Moon soil brought back by Apollo. They grew, but not as well as in Earth soil.
- 💡 Growing plants needs a lot of electricity for grow-lights, plus water and space. That's why, for short trips, carrying food is easier.
- 💚 Growing plants also helps astronauts feel happier and more at home, especially on long missions.

⚖️ Why it's a hard choice: a greenhouse costs power and water now, in exchange for fresh food, science and a happier crew later.

## 5. 📡 Communication delay: "Can we talk to Earth?" 🌍

💡 What it means: messages travel by radio at the speed of light. That's very fast, but space is very big.

### 🔬 Real facts:

- 🌍 A message takes about 1.3 seconds to reach the Moon from Earth, so a question and answer take about 2.5 seconds. That's like a slightly slow video call, so you can still chat,
- 🔴 For Mars it's 3 to 22 minutes each way, so no live chatting at all!

### 📵 The bigger problem on the Moon is blackouts, times when you can't reach Earth at all:

- 🌑 The far side of the Moon never faces Earth,
- 🏔️ At the south pole, Earth sits low in the sky, so hills can block the signal, like losing phone signal behind a building.
- 🚀 When Artemis Il flew behind the Moon in April 2026, the crew lost contact for about 40 minutes.
- 🛰️ Relay satellites fix this by passing messages around the Moon, like a postman. NASA, Europe (ESA) and Japan (JAXA) are agreeing shared rules called LunaNet, so everyone's satellites can work together, a bit like the internet.
- 🌐 Europe's Moonlight plans 5 satellites around the Moon by about 2028.
- 📸 On Artemis II, NASA tested a laser link that sent 484 gigabytes of photos, videos and data home. That's like thousands of phone photos.
- 🤖 In 2019 China's Chang'e 4 lander picked its own landing spot on the far side, because nobody on Earth could steer it live.

⚖️ Why it's a hard choice: in an emergency, the crew can't always wait for Mission Control. Sometimes they must decide on their own

## 🔗 How they all connect 🌕⚡

The five jobs are like a chain. Pull one and the others move:

- 🔬⚡ More power for science less power for the air machine → air tank drops
- ☀️☢️ A solar storm arrives → crew hides in shelter → no science that day
- 🌱💧 Build a greenhouse uses power and water→ less left for the battery
- 📵🌍 Radio blackout→ no help from Earth → crew must choose alone

That's why the challenge says "no choice is free". Every good decision costs something somewhere else, just like for real NASA mission planners.

🎮 In our game, players watch three gauges ( power, air and radiation dose). Food and communication appear as surprise events. That matches the challenge's advice to keep it to two or three things at once, so it stays fun and not confusing.
